# Delivery Reference

How to turn verified research into an answer that serves the reader. Read this before writing up any research longer than a direct answer.

## Match the shape to the question

The structure of the answer must come from the structure of the question, never from a fixed template. A reader asking one factual question and receiving a multi-section report has to excavate their answer; a reader asking for deep research and receiving three sentences has been given a verdict without the reasoning. Pick the smallest shape that carries the findings, and adjust or discard any part of the shapes below when the content calls for it.

Whatever the shape, lead with the conclusion. The strongest supported answer comes first; evidence, nuance, and caveats follow it. Readers should never have to reach the end to learn what you found.

## The common shapes

**Direct answer.** For a single fact or verdict: the answer in the first sentence, the key evidence and its source, the date it was confirmed, and any material caveat. No headings, no sections.

**Comparison matrix.** For multi-option questions: a table with options as columns and the agreed fields as rows, every cell either evidenced or visibly marked as unconfirmed, followed by a short reading of what the matrix implies for the user's stated criteria. The table carries the facts; the prose carries the judgment.

**Timeline.** For how-did-this-unfold questions: dated entries in event order, each anchored to a source, with unconfirmed items marked inline. Close with the current state as of the retrieval date.

**Research report.** For deep investigations: a summary of findings up front, sections that follow the natural structure of the findings, evidence with citations throughout, a section for open questions and conflicts if any survived verification, and a source list. Section headings should name findings ("Adoption is concentrated in two regions"), not process ("Search results").

**Monitoring brief.** For recurring updates: what changed since the last brief, in order of importance, each item dated and sourced; then a one-line statement for watched areas with no meaningful change. Keep the structure identical across briefs so differences stand out.

## Presenting citations

- Cite at the claim, not just in a list at the end. The reader should see, for each load-bearing claim, where it comes from, inline links where the host renders them, otherwise numbered references.
- Every answer with web-sourced claims ends with a source list: title, publisher or author, and URL, with the retrieval date where freshness matters.
- Cite the page that contains the claim, not the site's home page.
- When a claim rests on a secondary report of an original, either cite the original directly or make the chain visible.
- Do not cite what you did not read. A source that appeared in results but was never opened has no place in the list.

## Wording for certainty and uncertainty

Keep three levels of confidence distinguishable in the prose, so the reader can tell them apart without a legend:

- **Confirmed**: stated plainly, with source. "The change shipped in 4.2, released 2026-03-14."
- **Inferred**: marked as reasoning. "Both sources describe the same mechanism, which suggests..., though neither states it directly."
- **Unresolved**: named as open. "Whether this applies to the enterprise tier could not be confirmed; the official page does not address it."

Three wording rules protect this system. Do not let fluency inflate confidence: a sentence should sound exactly as certain as the evidence behind it. Reserve "latest", "current", and "as of now" for claims with a retrieval date attached. And when the honest answer is "could not be determined", write that, an explicit gap invites a better follow-up question, while a filled-in guess forecloses it.

## Coverage statements

Any answer that implies breadth, "all", "every", "the whole site", "across the market", must state what was actually covered: the inventory that was mapped, how much of it was read, and on what basis the rest was skipped. If coverage was a sample, the answer says it was a sample. This single sentence is the difference between a bounded claim a reader can trust and an unbounded claim they have to take on faith.

## Date discipline

- Resolve every relative time expression in the answer to a concrete date or range.
- Attach dates to facts that change: versions, prices, roles, statuses, standings.
- When sources conflict across time, present the dated sequence rather than only the newest claim, so the reader sees the change, not just its result.
- State the retrieval date for the answer as a whole when the topic is fast-moving.
